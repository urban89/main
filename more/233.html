Habitat Description

The Keyhole Cichlid (Cleithracara maronii) is native to the slow-moving rivers, streams, and floodplains of the Orinoco and Amazon basins in South America, particularly in Guyana and Venezuela. These waters are typically soft and slightly acidic, with sandy or muddy substrates and plenty of submerged vegetation and root structures for cover.

Scientific Facts

Cleithracara maronii belongs to the family Cichlidae and typically grows to about 10 cm (4 inches) in length. It is named for the distinctive black "keyhole" marking that appears on its side. The species was first described in 1905 and is not currently considered endangered (2024), although habitat degradation due to deforestation and pollution could pose future risks.

Life Style Summary

Keyhole Cichlids are peaceful, shy fish that prefer to live in pairs or small groups. They are bottom dwellers and thrive in well-planted aquariums with plenty of hiding spots. In the wild, they feed on small invertebrates, plant material, and detritus. In captivity, they do well on a varied diet of high-quality flakes, sinking pellets, and occasional live or frozen foods such as brine shrimp and bloodworms.

Interesting Fact

The Keyhole Cichlid is known for its strong pair-bonding behavior. Once a breeding pair is formed, the fish can exhibit long-term partnerships, with both the male and female sharing responsibilities in guarding and raising their fry.
