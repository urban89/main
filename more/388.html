Habitat Description

The Mayan Tetra (Hyphessobrycon compressus) is native to the freshwater rivers and streams of Central America, primarily in Mexico, Belize, and Guatemala. These fish inhabit slow-moving waters that are shaded by dense vegetation, with substrates composed of sand, leaf litter, and decaying organic material. The water in these environments is typically soft to moderately hard, with a pH range of 6.5 to 7.5, and temperatures between 24°C and 28°C (75°F to 82°F).

Scientific Facts

Belonging to the family Characidae, the Mayan Tetra reaches an average length of 4 to 6 cm (1.6 to 2.4 inches). As of 2024, it is not classified as endangered, although habitat destruction poses a potential risk to some populations. This species is known for its streamlined, laterally compressed body shape, which aids in navigating the dense aquatic vegetation of its native waters. The fish are prolific breeders, laying eggs that typically hatch within 24 to 36 hours.

Life Style Summary

Mayan Tetras are active and social fish that prefer to live in schools of six or more. They are primarily found in the middle to lower levels of the water column, constantly exploring their environment. In the wild, they feed on a variety of small invertebrates, plant detritus, and microorganisms. In captivity, they thrive on a diet consisting of quality flakes, small granules, and occasional live or frozen foods like bloodworms and brine shrimp.

Interesting Fact

It has a unique breeding behavior: males perform a series of rapid, darting movements to attract females, often leading them to areas with dense plant cover, which provides protection for their eggs. This behavior is thought to be an evolutionary adaptation to minimize predation risk during spawning.
